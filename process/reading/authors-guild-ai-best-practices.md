## Summary

Guidance for authors using AI (updated 2026-05-11; original 2024-02).
Six guiding principles (authors' voices define their work; AI output is
recombination of preexisting work; major LLMs trained on unlicensed
books; opposition to passing AI text off as human-written) and eleven
best practices: preserve authorship · use AI as background research
only, treating outputs like third-party sources · fact-check and review
for bias · disclose AI elements in copyright registration · get
publisher/agent approval in writing · ask for house AI rules · bind
third parties (editors, ghostwriters) on AI use · disclose
substantially-AI text to readers · don't mimic other writers' voices ·
consider displacement in narration/translation/illustration and prefer
legally trained models · caution on fine-tuning. Notes a "Human
Authored" certification mark. Key risks: AI text lacks
copyrightability; undisclosed AI content can invalidate registration
and breach manuscript warranties; LLMs can regurgitate copyrighted
material and hallucinate; public chatbots may train on inputs.

## What it argues

Human authorship is the professional and legal foundation; AI use is
acceptable in support roles (research, mechanics) with verification and
transparency; the reader's right to know is central; authors bear full
responsibility for what they deliver.

  real sources corroborate; the disclosure stance (principles §1,
  CONTRIBUTING.md) covers practices 4–6 and 8.

  not adopted here): explicit bias review in the researcher/coverage
  ethic; a no-style-mimicry principle (AI never imitates other living
  writers' voices); solidarity considerations for future production
  phases (narration, translation, cover art; prefer legally trained
  models); pursue the Human Authored certification — the repo's
  receipts are purpose-built evidence for it.
